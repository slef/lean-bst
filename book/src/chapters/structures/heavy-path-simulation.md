# Heavy paths

The deamortization of [Chapter 24](deamortization.html) runs an arbitrary source BST algorithm inside a tree that it keeps balanced in a weighted sense. Whatever shape the source tree takes, every key $i$ stays at depth $O(\log(W/w_i))$ in the tree that is actually stored, where $w_i$ is the key's weight and $W$ the total weight. This is Theorem 5 of Bose, Collette, Fagerberg and Langerman [-@bose2012]. The simulation stores the source tree through its *heavy-path decomposition*: each node has a solid edge to its heavier child, and each maximal solid path is kept as a pair of the pop-tarts of [Chapter 31](poptarts.html). The subtrees hanging off a path are stored inside the pop-tarts as weighted leaves, and they are represented in the same way, recursively.

The paper (Section 3) simulates a source tree $T$, whose finger moves and rotates, by a tree $T'$ whose finger stays at the root. The library splits this into four steps, one section each.

- **[Section 32.1](heavy-paths.html): one heavy path.** The decomposition into solid and light edges, the canonical heavy path from the root, and the *paired* representation of one path ([[HeavyPath.Paired.Stacks]]). It provides pushes, which add the next ancestor to the path, and exposures, which pop the topmost ancestor out of it. Both come with exact pointer traces, amortized costs, worst-case costs and depth bounds, including depth bounds at every intermediate step.
- **[Chapter 47](recursive-heavy-paths.html): recursive heavy paths.** [[HeavyPath.Recursive.rebuild]] applies the paired representation recursively: every off-path payload is itself rebuilt as a pair, down to the leaves. With positive weights, every key ends at depth at most $7 + 15\log_2(W/w)$ ([[HeavyPath.Recursive.Results.rebuild_depth]]), and building the layout from any initial tree costs at most $16n$ pointer operations ([[HeavyPath.Recursive.Results.rebuild_initialization]]). The *history* variant ([[HeavyPath.Recursive.History.Layout]]) allows arbitrary valid pop-tart histories at every level, carries repair credits, and supports exposing the source root and joining it back. This is the paper's situation "when its finger is at the root of $T$".
- **[Chapter 48](heavy-path-fingers.html): fingers on heavy paths.** When the source finger $f$ is not at the root, the path from $f$ up to the root is stored *upside down* as another pair of pop-tarts ([[HeavyPath.Finger.Pair]]), the paper's Figure 5, so that the physical root is the finger node. Ancestors are charged to the next payload toward the finger. The section proves the analogous depth bound, $15 + 15\log_2(W/w)$, and the transfers that move the finger one step down by moving one node from one pop-tart of the pair to the other.
- **[Chapter 49](finger-histories.html): finger histories and simulation.** [[HeavyPath.Finger.History.State]] gathers all the physical payloads around the finger, each with its own recursive layout and credit, under one invariant. A *policy* answers each of the six source instructions (moves and rotations) with a physical program that keeps the invariant. Its potential pays the amortized cost, its responses are logarithmic in the worst case, and the key depths stay bounded at every interruption. This is what [[Deamortization.Weighted.Results.simulation]] packages.

The first section provides the vocabulary that the other three use: weights and masses, frames and their charges, the pair of stacks, and the substitution of payloads.

The theorem of the last section is what the deamortization uses, as [[Deamortization.Weighted.Results.simulation]]. A reader who only wants to understand that statement needs the decomposition of [Section 32.1](heavy-paths.html#the-heavy-path-decomposition) and the policies of [Section 32.4](finger-histories.html); the rest is the proof.
